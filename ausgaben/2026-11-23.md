## Chesterton's fence
*Before you remove a rule or structure, find out why someone built it.*

### What it is
A principle about change. You find a fence across a road and see no use for it. Chesterton says: do not tear it down until you know why it was put up. A team that drops an odd approval step, without asking why it exists, may bring back the exact problem the step solved.

### Where it comes from
G. K. Chesterton, an English essayist, wrote it in "The Thing" (1929). He was arguing about reformers who attack institutions they do not understand. The name "Chesterton's fence" came later, from commentators.

### Why it came up
Farnam Street's article retells the parable and applies it to companies, laws and habits. It stresses that the rule is not a defence of the status quo. It asks for understanding first and reform second.

### Key ideas
- Not seeing the purpose of a thing is not proof it has none.
- The test is a question: who built this, and what problem did they face?
- After you know the reason, you may still remove the fence.
- Misused, the rule becomes an excuse to never change anything.

**Source:** [Chesterton (1929), The Thing, ch. IV "The Drift from Domesticity" (text copy)](https://gist.github.com/austintaylor/90c9b867ca949d7ffb748549a76422bb), book

**Surfaced in:** [Farnam Street, Chesterton's Fence](https://fs.blog/chestertons-fence/)

## Clinger's fast path
*A shortcut for turning decimal text into a float that is provably still exact.*

### What it is
Computers store decimals like 0.1 as binary floating-point, so reading "12.34" means finding the nearest binary value. The fast path covers easy cases. If the digits form an integer small enough to be exact (up to 2^53) and the power of ten is small (up to 10^22), one multiplication or division gives the correctly rounded answer. Only harder inputs need slow big-integer arithmetic.

### Where it comes from
William Clinger published it in 1990 at PLDI, in "How to Read Floating Point Numbers Accurately". Before it, many systems rounded wrongly when parsing. His observation was that rounding twice can differ from rounding once, which is the core difficulty.

### Why it came up
Simon Willison compared C and Rust SQLite extensions that parse numbers. The first C version, using strtod, was slower on decimals. He asked Claude to write a faster parser, and it used Clinger's fast path. His post reports about 18 ns per decimal row, against 32 to 55 ns for the Rust versions.

### Key ideas
- Two roundings can give a different result than one rounding.
- Most real-world numbers are short, so the exact cheap case is common.
- When the cheap case fails, fall back to a slower correct method.
- Correct reading was later made much faster by successors, but this idea persists.

**Source:** [Clinger (1990), How to Read Floating Point Numbers Accurately, PLDI](https://doi.org/10.1145/93548.93557), paper

**Surfaced in:** [Simon Willison, parse_number() as a SQLite extension: C vs Rust](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Nov/8/sqlite-parse-number-c-vs-rust/)

**Related:** IEEE 754, Eisel-Lemire algorithm

## Weight-space programming
*Treating a neural network's raw weights as code an LLM can read and edit.*

### What it is
Normally an optimizer sets a network's weights. Here a general LLM reads the numbers directly and says what the network does. In one test it told which of four games a policy had been trained on. It can also edit weights. For a failing driving policy it changed 3.5% of the weights and reached 100% success.

### Where it comes from
Rikiya Takehi, Jayden Teoh, Alex Zhang and Omar Khattab (MIT CSAIL) coined the term in a paper dated 2 November 2026. They asked whether an LLM can reason about weights as a programmer reasons about source code, with no training loop.

### Why it came up
Willison reran one of the paper's prompts on a local Qwen 3.8 27B model. It named Pac-Man, Pong and Breakout with 72% to 88% stated confidence but missed Freeway. He thinks the wider commentary goes too far.

### Key ideas
- Weights are a program, and an LLM can be the reader and writer.
- Editing removed obstacles that hid abilities, but did not add missing ones.
- The authors call it early evidence of a capability, not a validated method.
- Some tasks still needed tools, such as inspecting attention patterns.

**Source:** [Takehi et al. (2026), Sparks of Weight-Space Programming](https://rikiyat.github.io/blog/weight-space-programming/), paper

**Surfaced in:** [Simon Willison, Qwen 3.8 27B on my laptop can interpret model weights, too](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Nov/3/qwen-can-interpret-model-weights/)

**Related:** Mechanistic interpretability, Model editing
