## Liquid types
*Types that carry logical facts, such as "this integer is never negative", inferred automatically.*

### What it is
A normal type says "this is an integer". A liquid type says "this is an integer v with 0 ≤ v". The compiler works out such facts itself, from a short list of candidate conditions. In the authors' example, a small ML program that sums absolute values over a list is proven to return a non-negative number.

### Where it comes from
Patrick Rondon, Ming Kawaguchi and Ranjit Jhala at UC San Diego presented it in 2008 (a first report by Rondon and Jhala appeared in 2007). Earlier dependent types could prove such facts, but programmers had to write every detail by hand. Liquid types keep ML-style inference and add automatic logic checking.

### Why it came up
A Hacker News post argues that software needs a mainstream language that can be formally verified. Its author plans to start with liquid types, powered by an SMT solver, and add a proof assistant later. The idea is to begin easy and grow stronger, not the reverse.

### Key ideas
- A type is refined with a logical predicate over its value.
- The programmer supplies only a small set of "qualifiers"; the system picks which ones hold where.
- Normal ML type inference gives the overall shape; predicate abstraction fills in the facts.
- Checking stays decidable, so it can run like a compiler pass.

**Source:** [Rondon, Jhala (2007), Liquid Types: Type Refinement via Predicate Abstraction](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/0vx7j8zc), paper

**Surfaced in:** [The need for a formally verifiable mainstream programming language (Hacker News thread)](https://blainehansen.me/post/mainstream-verifiable-language/)

**Related:** Refinement types, predicate abstraction

## Aleatoric vs. epistemic uncertainty
*Two kinds of "I'm not sure": built-in randomness versus a gap in knowledge.*

### What it is
Aleatoric uncertainty is randomness no extra information removes: a fair coin stays 50/50 however much you study it. Epistemic uncertainty is missing knowledge: you do not know a Swahili word yet, but the answer exists and can be learned. The first is irreducible; the second shrinks with more data.

### Where it comes from
The words come from Latin and Greek roots (alea, dice; episteme, knowledge). Machine learning long treated all uncertainty as plain probability. Hüllermeier and Waegeman's 2019 survey gathered the distinction for learning systems, driven by safety needs, where a model should tell "this is noisy" from "I have not seen this".

### Why it came up
An introduction to uncertainty in machine learning explains that a prediction is not a decision, since decisions weigh outcomes by likelihood and cost. It uses the same split, and notes that a fact counts as aleatoric only relative to what the model can see. Measuring the coin throw turns chance into knowledge.

### Key ideas
- Aleatoric is irreducible; epistemic is reducible with more data.
- The line is context-dependent: adding a feature can turn noise into signal.
- Epistemic splits into approximation uncertainty (not enough data) and model uncertainty (wrong model family).
- Model uncertainty is usually ignored because it is hard to capture.

**Source:** [Hüllermeier, Waegeman (2021), Aleatoric and Epistemic Uncertainty in Machine Learning](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.09457), paper

**Surfaced in:** [An introduction to uncertainty in machine learning](https://chrhenning.com/blog/2026/introduction-to-uncertainty/)

**Related:** Bayesian inference, conformal prediction

## Disconfirmed expectancy
*When a clear prophecy fails, believers may believe harder and recruit more.*

### What it is
It describes what happens when a strongly held belief is plainly refuted. Instead of giving up, some people double down and try to convince others. Example: a group predicts the end of the world on a set date, the date passes, and members start spreading the message harder.

### Where it comes from
Leon Festinger, Henry Riecken and Stanley Schachter infiltrated a small Chicago group that expected a flood in 1954 and wrote it up in 1956. They wanted to test cognitive dissonance: people resolve the clash between belief and facts by changing the story, not the belief. They set out five conditions for this effect.

### Why it came up
The formal-verification post warns against betting everything on an unrealized future and cites the Great Disappointment, when a predicted 1844 return of Christ did not happen. The post's point is that waiting for a future fix, such as better AI, is risky. That episode is the classic case of a failed date.

### Key ideas
- The belief is held with deep conviction and bears on action.
- The believer has committed, with actions that are hard to undo.
- The belief is specific enough that events can refute it, and the refutation is recognised.
- Social support from fellow believers is needed to keep going.

**Source:** [Festinger, Riecken, Schachter (1956), When Prophecy Fails, ch. 1](https://archive.org/details/when.prophecy.fails), book

**Surfaced in:** [The need for a formally verifiable mainstream programming language (Hacker News thread)](https://blainehansen.me/post/mainstream-verifiable-language/)

**Related:** Cognitive dissonance, belief perseverance
