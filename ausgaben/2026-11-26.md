## Context of discovery vs. context of justification
*Where an idea comes from is a different question from whether it is true.*

### What it is
Philosophers of science split a theory's life in two. The context of discovery is how someone came up with the idea. The context of justification is how it is tested and defended. Kekulé may have dreamed of a snake biting its tail before proposing the benzene ring. The dream is irrelevant to whether the ring structure is right.

### Where it comes from
Hans Reichenbach drew the line in *Experience and Prediction* (1938). The terms are often credited to him, though the reference entry notes his own view was subtler than the popular version. For much of the 20th century, the split let philosophy treat only justification as its business. Discovery was left to psychology and sociology.

### Why it came up
Saloni Dattani argues that famous "accidental" discoveries (Fleming's penicillin, Pasteur's vaccine) were told as neat stories long after the fact. The notes and letters show deliberate work. Her piece is about the gap between how a discovery is retold and how it happened. That gap is what the two contexts name.

### Key ideas
- Generating a hypothesis and checking it are different activities, even for the same theory.
- They need not be separate stages in time. They are different questions about the same idea.
- Strict separation made discovery look like a mystery. Later philosophers pushed back and studied it.
- Whewell: a "happy thought" is not a wild guess. A prepared mind is needed to notice the facts.

**Source:** [Schickore (rev. 2022), Scientific Discovery, Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/scientific-discovery/), reference

## Linear scan register allocation
*A compiler assigns CPU registers in one quick pass instead of solving a graph puzzle.*

### What it is
A CPU has few registers, and a program has many variables. The compiler must decide who gets a register and who is "spilled" to slow memory. Linear scan lists each variable's live range, then walks through the ranges once, in order. When registers run out, it spills the variable whose range ends last.

### Where it comes from
Massimiliano Poletto and Vivek Sarkar published it in 1999, after a 1997 synopsis. The standard method then was graph coloring (Chaitin, 1981). It gives good code but gets slow on big functions. Linear scan was meant for just-in-time compilers, where compile time counts as run time.

### Why it came up
Max Bernstein and Yannis Smaragdakis wrote the whole algorithm in Datalog, a declarative logic language. The post says Datalog suits liveness analysis well. It fits the step-by-step, stateful scan badly, so they emulate loops with tricks such as a "forall" over registers.

### Key ideas
- Each variable becomes an interval: first definition to last use.
- One greedy pass replaces building a big interference graph.
- Spilling picks the interval that ends furthest away, echoing Belady's cache-replacement rule.
- The paper reports code within 12% of graph coloring on most benchmarks, with much faster compilation.

**Source:** [Poletto & Sarkar (1999), Linear Scan Register Allocation](https://www.cse.iitm.ac.in/~krishna/cs3300/linearscan.pdf), paper

**Surfaced in:** [Bernstein, Linear scan register allocation in Datalog](https://bernsteinbear.com/blog/lsra-datalog/)

**Related:** Graph-coloring register allocation, JIT compilation

## VP vs. VNP
*Valiant's arithmetic twin of P vs. NP, with the permanent as the hard case.*

### What it is
Take a polynomial, built only from adding and multiplying. VP holds the polynomials with small circuits of such operations. VNP holds those written as a sum over exponentially many 0/1 choices. A matrix's determinant and its permanent are almost the same sum. The determinant has minus signs, and it is easy. The permanent has none, and no small circuit is known.

### Where it comes from
Leslie Valiant defined both classes in a short 1979 conference paper. He showed the permanent is complete for VNP, so it is the hardest case. He conjectured VP is not equal to VNP. That mirrors P vs. NP but works with algebra instead of bits.

### Why it came up
A Hacker News post points to a claimed proof that VP is not VNP, and explains the permanent and determinant in three minutes. I could not check that claim, and the surveyed source below still calls the conjecture open. The post is a good short tour of the two classes anyway.

### Key ideas
- Algebraic complexity counts additions and multiplications, not bit operations.
- Determinant vs. permanent differs only by signs, yet one is easy and one is not known to be.
- The permanent is VNP-complete: if it is easy, everything in VNP is easy.
- Bürgisser calls the conjecture "widely open and considered the holy grail" of the field.

**Source:** [Bürgisser (2024), Completeness classes in algebraic complexity theory, overview of Valiant (1979)](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.06217v1), paper

**Surfaced in:** [What VP ≠ VNP Means (In 3 Minutes) (Hacker News)](https://www.friedgil.com/vpvnp)

**Related:** P vs. NP, arithmetic circuits
